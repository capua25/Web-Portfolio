## The brief

A client came with an idea: a platform to manage sporting events — registrations
and results.

Put that way, it sounds like a CRUD app with a form. Taken apart, it isn't.

**The people getting paid aren't one, they're many.** Each club owns its money.
A platform that holds it stops being software and becomes a financial
intermediary, with everything that implies. It's not a checkout detail: it
defines the entire payment model, and it has to be settled before writing the
first line of code.

**A spot is a contested resource.** Two people signing up at the same time for
the last place is not an edge case: it's the normal case on the day registration
opens for a popular race. Concurrency doesn't trickle in. It arrives all at
once, the minute the link is published.

**The system doesn't produce the results.** An external timing system,
Webscorer, does. The platform doesn't time anything: it reads. That means
integrating with a foreign format without letting it leak into the platform's
own data model, because the day the vendor changes something, the domain can't
move with it.

**Money comes in through channels that aren't cards.** In Uruguay the typical
case is a RedPagos or Abitab ticket: issued, numbered, and not yet paid. Payment
can be completed hours later, at a counter. That rewrites the payment window and
retry logic: "hasn't paid yet" can't be treated as "won't pay".

Five months later, the platform is in production: Carmelo Eventos Deportivos
uses it to collect registrations for the Carmelo Trail Run 2026.

## What I built

**Registration and payment.** Checkout runs on MercadoPago and handles a case
that comes up more often than you'd expect: registration as a gift, where the
person paying isn't the person running. Each registration also gets a bib number
that's unique within the event, which is the field results are later matched
against.

  <Image

  />
  <figcaption>

  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Multi-account payments.** Each organization connects its own MercadoPago
account via OAuth. Registration money goes 100% to the organizer:
`marketplace_fee: 0`. The platform is never part of the flow of funds.

<figure>
  <Image
    src={panelOrganizador}
    alt="Organizer dashboard: their event listed with spots and registrants, and the MercadoPago connection block showing their own account already linked."
  />
  <figcaption>
    Organizer dashboard — development environment, test data. Organizers
    self-manage: their event, their registrants and their own MercadoPago
    account.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Results.** Times are synced from Webscorer, the external timing system. The
integration is read-only and runs on a cron every two minutes. Times are stored
as an integer number of milliseconds (`tiempoMs`), never as a string: a time
string sorts badly lexicographically, and the day someone wants a podium, the
podium comes out wrong.

**Championships.** Several dates make up a championship. Each event awards
points per placement, and points are credited when each date closes, not at the
end. That way the standings are always live.

All of this is exposed through four dashboards for different audiences: the
public one (anyone who comes to look at an event), the registered user's, the
organizer's and the admin's. Each has its own server-side guard layer. None of
them depends on the frontend hiding a button.

## The decisions

<DecisionCard
  titulo="Each club gets paid into its own account"
  descarte="A single platform account with manual settlement"
>
  If registration money lands in an account of mine and I later forward it to
  the club, I stop being a software provider and start holding third-party
  funds. That's a different business, with different obligations. I preferred
  that the platform never touch the money: `marketplace_fee: 0`.
  <Fragment slot="detalle">
    Each organization connects its account through OAuth. The flow's `state` is
    signed with HMAC-SHA256, expires after 10 minutes and is bound to the
    `organizacionId` resolved by the guard, not the one the client sends. Tokens
    are stored encrypted with AES-256-GCM in a separate table
    (`OrganizacionMercadoPago`), not as columns of `Organizacion`: that way an
    `include` without a projection can't leak a third party's access token.

    A single factory (`MercadoPagoClientFactory.forOrganizacion()`) resolves the
    credentials and fails explicitly if there are none, if they're revoked or if
    they expire in less than 7 days. **No silent fallback to a global account.**
    The consequence is accepted: if an organization hasn't connected its
    account, it can't publish paid events. No exceptions, not even for the
    platform's own organization.

  </Fragment>
</DecisionCard>

<DecisionCard
  titulo="Idempotency in five layers, not one"
  descarte="Relying on a single deduplication mechanism"
>
  A payment webhook can arrive twice, late, or out of order. That's not a
  hypothesis: it's the normal contract of any payment provider. Charging someone
  twice is one of those mistakes that don't get forgiven, so I didn't want to
  rest that guarantee on a single mechanism.
  <Fragment slot="detalle">
    The five layers: (1) a deterministic `jobId` derived from the MercadoPago
    payment id; (2) a terminal-state guard, which stops processing if the entity
    has already reached a final state; (3) the `external_reference` versioned by
    timestamp, shaped as `${tipo}:${id}:${createdAt.getTime()}`; (4) all writes
    conditioned on state — `updateMany` with `where: { estado: X }`, and a
    `count === 0` is treated as "another process won", not as an error; (5)
    `idempotencyKey` on refunds against the SDK.

    The third one is the most interesting, and I learned it the hard way.
    `Pago.inscripcionId` is `@unique`, so reviving a cancelled registration
    recycles the same `Pago` row. Without the timestamp inside the
    `external_reference`, a late notification from an already-dead payment
    attempt was processed as if it belonged to the new attempt. The identifier
    was stable, and that stability was exactly the problem.

  </Fragment>
</DecisionCard>

<DecisionCard
  titulo="A lock on the spot, not an optimistic count"
  descarte="Count registrants and then create (count-then-create)"
>
  Counting and then inserting seems reasonable until two requests arrive
  together: both read the same count, both conclude there's one spot left, and
  both create. Overselling. On the day a popular race opens, that isn't
  hypothetical, it's what's going to happen.
  <Fragment slot="detalle">
    Every registration creation goes through `createWithCupoLock()`, which takes
    a `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` on the event row inside the transaction, before
    counting. The spot stops being a number you read and becomes a resource you
    acquire.

    It's documented as one of the invariants the code deliberately protects,
    along with three others: a single definition of "active registration" (not
    reimplemented in every query), the backend as the only source of truth for
    the amount to charge (`montoParaUsuario()`), and price and capacity frozen
    once there are registrants.

  </Fragment>
</DecisionCard>

<DecisionCard
  titulo="Scoping lives in its own table, not in a User FK"
  descarte="Adding an organizacionId to Usuario"
>
  `Usuario` already relates to `Organizacion` through `Membresia`, and that
  relation means "is a member of". A second foreign key meaning "manages" made
  the schema ambiguous: two paths between the same two tables, with different
  meanings. Sooner or later someone was going to take the wrong one.
  <Fragment slot="detalle">
    I created an `Organizador` table as the single source of truth for scoping.
    The N:M came as a bonus with no future migration: several people managing one
    club, or one production company managing several clubs, already fit in the
    model.

    On top of it sits the `OrganizerGuard`, which **never** trusts an
    `organizacionId` coming from the JWT or the client, and which throws a 500
    instead of returning `undefined` if it can't resolve it. That's deliberate: a
    tenant filter that silently degrades to an unscoped query is worse than an
    outage, because it returns someone else's data and nobody notices.

    In addition, `Usuario` and `Admin` are completely separate tables and
    authentication systems: two JWT secrets, two token lifetimes, two cookie
    pairs. A user token isn't a downgraded admin token; it's something else.

  </Fragment>
</DecisionCard>

<DecisionCard
  titulo="Points are stored one at a time, never summed"
  descarte="Storing each participant's running total"
>
  A total is a number with no history. When someone complains that they're
  missing points in the championship standings — and someone always complains —
  without the detail there's no way to audit where they came from.
  <Fragment slot="detalle">
    `MovimientoScoring` is an append-only ledger: each credit is stored as a
    row, and the total is aggregated in the query. From the design document
    itself: _"if it were stored already summed, the origin of those points would
    be lost forever"_.
  </Fragment>
</DecisionCard>

There's one more decision that doesn't make it to a card but is worth
mentioning. `packages/shared` centralizes the domain enums and the response
envelopes, and **deliberately doesn't depend on `@prisma/client`**, because the
frontend doesn't have it. So it declares the enums on its own, and a test
(`enum-sync.spec.ts`) guarantees the two sides don't diverge, in types or at
runtime. It's controlled duplication, verified by a test, instead of coupling
the frontend build to the backend's ORM.

## Three things that went wrong

<HistoriaDeGuerra
  titulo="The bug that stayed silent for four months"
  sintoma="Payments that MercadoPago confirmed and the system never recorded."
  causaRaiz="A malformed jobId that BullMQ rejected inside queue.add(), where no test that mocks the queue can see it."
>
  BullMQ rejects a `jobId` that contains `:` and doesn't resolve to exactly three
  segments. And it rejects it inside `queue.add()` — that is, in the one place no
  test that mocks the queue ever reaches. A malformed identifier left the
  MercadoPago webhook broken for four months. Silently. On the money path.

The fix wasn't correcting that identifier. It was centralizing the construction
of all ids in a single function, `buildJobId()`. A bug that survives four months
isn't fixed where it showed up: it's fixed where it could show up again.

</HistoriaDeGuerra>

<HistoriaDeGuerra
  titulo="Four chained failures in production"
  sintoma="The frontend container marked unhealthy and the file upload system broken."
  causaRaiz="Four independent problems stacked up: server bind, localhost resolution, a missing nginx location and volume permissions."
>
  All four, in order.

**First.** Next.js's `server.js` in standalone mode binds to
`process.env.HOSTNAME`, and Docker injects the container id there. The server
started listening on an address that didn't exist.

**Second.** With the bind fixed to `0.0.0.0` — which is IPv4 only — the
healthcheck kept failing, because inside the container `localhost` also resolves
to `::1`.

**Third.** nginx had no `location /uploads/`, so those routes fell through to
the catch-all.

**Fourth.** The uploads volume was mounted `root:root` and NestJS runs as the
`nestjs` user. EACCES.

Four fixes, verified in production. The lesson I took away is short and I've
applied it without thinking ever since: write healthchecks against `127.0.0.1`,
never against `localhost`.

</HistoriaDeGuerra>

<Callout tipo="alerta">
  That same class of error — frontend route handlers missing their `location` in
  nginx, falling through to the catch-all and producing 401s and 404s
  disconnected from the real cause — happened five times. One of those times it
  escalated badly: I read a 401 as a form bug and ended up disabling an entire
  feature instead of fixing nginx. I'm telling it straight because it's the kind
  of mistake that teaches more than a success: the symptom was in the
  application, the cause was one layer below, and I kept looking where the light
  was.
</Callout>

<HistoriaDeGuerra
  titulo="What MercadoPago doesn't document"
  sintoma="Users abandoning checkout with no apparent cause."
  causaRaiz="If the payer's email matches an existing MercadoPago account, Checkout Pro forces a login and stops offering guest payment."
>
  MercadoPago doesn't document this. It was confirmed empirically, by trying
  different emails until the pattern was obvious: the same checkout behaves
  differently depending on who opens it, and whoever already has an account
  loses the option to pay as a guest.

When the provider doesn't document something, the only way out is to reproduce
it and measure it. There's no way to reason it out from the outside.

</HistoriaDeGuerra>

## What I'd do differently

**There's no CI.** 1,433 commits and 209 test files — 100 with Jest in the API,
109 with Vitest in the frontend — and no pipeline that runs them automatically.
It's the project's most visible gap: the tests exist, but running them depends
on someone remembering. A test that doesn't run on its own is documentation with
syntax.

**Minimal observability.** The only alert channel is an email via Postmark. No
Sentry, no uptime monitor. If something breaks at night, I find out when I open
it in the morning.

**Leftovers.** Things left half-done that the architecture document itself
lists: Stripe support declared in the configuration without a single line of
integration, i18n infrastructure set up with no component consuming it, a
revalidation endpoint nobody calls, a queue job that does nothing. Documenting
them was more useful than pretending they aren't there: what's written down can
be deleted, what's hidden gets inherited.

**Deployment is manual.** Docker and nginx on a self-managed VPS. It ended up
accumulating commits made directly on the server, which later had to be
reconciled. And a certificate expired because of a badly configured renewal
setup.

<Callout tipo="dato">
  The counterweight, which is also verifiable: an external black-box pentest in
  May 2026 gained no access. The medium-severity findings were missing HTTP
  headers. Today a `curl -I https://pinarsports.com` returns HSTS with `preload`
  and CSP. Closed, and verifiable by anyone from the outside.
</Callout>
